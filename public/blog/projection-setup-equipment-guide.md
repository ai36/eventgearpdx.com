# Projection Setup and Equipment: What It Actually Takes to Get It Right

![a group of people sitting around a conference table - EventGear PDX](/blog/images/projection-setup-equipment-guide/cover.jpg)

> A practical look at what goes into a working projection setup — projector brightness, screen sizing, cabling, and the on-site testing that prevents a presentation from falling apart.

You've got a presentation coming up in a room that wasn't built for one — a conference room with no built-in display, a rented hall, a backyard tent for a client dinner or a family celebration. Someone has to figure out the projector, the screen, and how it all connects to a laptop, and that someone is usually not an AV technician. It sounds simple until you're standing in the room an hour before doors open.

## Brightness Has to Match the Room, Not Just the Projector

A projector rated at 2,000 lumens looks fine in a demo video and washes out completely under afternoon windows or venue house lights. Lumens needed scale with ambient light, not room size — a dim conference room might be fine at 2,500 lumens, while a space with large windows or string lighting needs 4,000 or more before text and photos read clearly from the back row.

## Screen Size and Throw Distance Are a Package Deal

Screen size isn't a preference, it's math: throw distance (how far the projector sits from the screen) determines the image size a given projector can produce, and viewing distance determines the minimum size that's actually readable. A screen that looks plenty big on paper can still be undersized if the back of the room sits 40 feet away, or oversized and blurry if the projector is mounted too close.

## Cabling and Adapters Are Where Things Actually Fail

Most projection problems on event day aren't the projector itself — they're a laptop with only USB-C ports meeting a projector that only takes HDMI, or a last-minute presenter whose adapter doesn't match anyone's cable. Add a second presenter, a video clip with audio, or a hybrid setup streaming to remote attendees, and the chain of connections gets long enough that one missing adapter stalls the whole room.

When any of this goes wrong, it goes wrong in front of the audience. A washed-out slide deck, a frozen laptop screen, a fifteen-minute delay while someone hunts for the right cable — none of it is a minor inconvenience when the room is full of clients, investors, or guests who showed up on time. The cost isn't the equipment, it's the credibility of the event itself.

## Matching Equipment to the Room Solves Most of This Before It Starts

Getting projection right means selecting projector brightness for the actual light conditions in that specific room, sizing the screen to both throw and viewing distance, and bringing every adapter the connection chain could need — not just the one that's supposed to work. ## A Technician On Site Removes the Single Point of Failure

The difference between a projection setup that holds up and one that doesn't usually comes down to whether someone is watching it during the event. A technician on site for the full event can swap a cable, adjust focus as the room fills and light changes, or route a second presenter's laptop without the audience noticing anything happened at all.

## FAQ

**How many lumens do I need for a daytime event with windows?**
For a room with significant daylight or uncovered windows, aim for 4,000 lumens or more. A fully darkened room can work with 2,000–2,500 lumens, but most venues fall somewhere in between, which is why matching brightness to the specific room matters more than picking a number off a spec sheet.

**What screen size do I actually need?**
A rough rule is one inch of diagonal screen for every foot of viewing distance to the farthest seat. A room with guests 30 feet back needs at least a 100-inch screen regardless of how small the venue feels up front.

**Can I just use a laptop's built-in HDMI without a technician?**
You can, until the laptop only has USB-C, the cable run is longer than the included adapter supports, or a second presenter shows up with a different device. Testing the full chain before the event is what catches this, not the projector itself.

**Do you provide screens as well as projectors, or just the projector?**
EventGear PDX rents projectors and screens together, sized to the room and throw distance, along with the cabling and adapters the event needs.

**Can projection work outdoors, like a backyard or park event?**
Yes, with a projector bright enough for ambient light and a screen that can be anchored or weighted against wind. Timing also matters — outdoor projection reads best once the sun is low or down.

**How far in advance should I book equipment for a presentation or event?**
As early as the event date is known. Availability depends on the rental schedule, and booking early gives time to confirm the room's layout, lighting, and screen size before the event rather than guessing on the day.

## The Bottom Line

A projection setup that works is less about owning a projector and more about matching brightness to the room, sizing the screen to the actual viewing distance, and having every cable the laptop chain could need — then having someone on site to catch the one thing that wasn't anticipated. Get those pieces right and the technology disappears into the background, which is exactly where it belongs during someone else's presentation.
