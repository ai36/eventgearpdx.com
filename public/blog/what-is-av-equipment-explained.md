# What Is AV Equipment? A Plain-English Guide for Event Organizers

![people sitting on chair - EventGear PDX](/blog/images/what-is-av-equipment-explained/cover.jpg)

> A practical breakdown of what AV equipment actually includes — projectors, screens, sound systems, microphones — and how to figure out what your event actually needs.

You've been put in charge of a company all-hands, a wedding ceremony, or a parent night at school, and someone on the planning committee just said "we'll need to sort out the AV." If you're not sure what that actually means beyond "a microphone, maybe," you're not alone — most people organizing an event for the first time have never had to think about it before.

## So What Does "AV Equipment" Actually Cover?

AV stands for audio-visual, and in practice it breaks into a handful of categories. Audio covers speakers, subwoofers, mixers, and microphones — wireless handhelds, lavalier clip-ons, or podium mics. Visual covers projectors, screens, and increasingly monitors or displays for smaller rooms. There's also a growing third category: streaming gear — cameras and audio feeds that pipe your event to Zoom or Teams for people who can't attend in person. None of this is interchangeable. A speaker that fills a 30-person conference room will sound thin in a 300-person hall, and a projector bright enough for a dim ballroom will wash out completely under afternoon sun at an outdoor ceremony.

## The Problem: Most Spaces Aren't Built for This

Here's the catch — almost none of the venues people actually use for one-off events have any of this built in. A rented community hall has house lights and maybe an outlet near the stage, nothing else. An office conference room has a TV meant for six people, not sixty. A backyard has neither. So the question isn't just "what equipment do I need" — it's "what does this specific room, with its specific size, layout, and acoustics, actually require to make speech and images reach every seat?"

## What Happens When You Guess Wrong

Guessing wrong here isn't a minor inconvenience. A single speaker pointed at a long, narrow room leaves the back third straining to hear the toast or the CEO's remarks. A consumer-grade projector in a space with any ambient light produces a washed-out, hard-to-read slide deck during the exact ten minutes that matter — the product demo, the investor pitch. And a wireless mic with interference from a nearby Wi-Fi network cutting in and out mid-ceremony is the kind of thing nobody forgets, including the couple whose vows it happened during.

## Matching Equipment to the Actual Event

The fix isn't buying the most powerful gear available — it's matching equipment to the room and the group size. A 50-person training session needs a mid-size PA speaker and one or two lavalier mics, not a concert rig. A wedding ceremony for 80 guests outdoors needs weather-appropriate speakers and a mic built for vows and toasts, plus someone who can run a quick soundcheck before anyone arrives. This is also where having a technician present for the whole event, not just a drop-off, matters: equipment that's tested and working at 9am can still need a quick adjustment at 2pm once the room fills with people and their coats absorb sound differently than an empty hall.

## FAQ

**Do I need a projector and a screen, or just a TV?**
For rooms under about 20 people, a large TV or monitor is often enough. Past that, a projector and screen sized to the room reads better from the back and holds up against ambient light far more reliably than a consumer display.

**How many microphones does a typical event need?**
It depends on the format. A single speaker giving a presentation needs one lavalier or handheld mic; a panel or a wedding with readings and toasts usually needs two to four, plus a backup.

**Can I just rent equipment and run it myself?**
You can with some consumer gear, but matching speaker placement, mic frequencies, and projector brightness to a specific room takes some experience — a wrong guess usually only shows up once the room is full.

**What's the difference between a PA system and "just speakers"?**
A PA system is the full chain — speakers, an amplifier or powered speakers, a mixer, and inputs for microphones — built to carry speech clearly to a crowd. A pair of standalone speakers without that chain usually can't take a microphone input cleanly.

**Does outdoor AV need different equipment than indoor?**
Yes. Outdoor speakers need more power since there are no walls to contain the sound, and wireless mics need to be chosen carefully to avoid interference in open areas with lots of other wireless signals nearby.

## The Bottom Line

AV equipment isn't one thing — it's audio, visual, and increasingly streaming gear, each sized to a specific room and group. Most venues used for one-off events have none of it built in, which means the planning question is really about matching gear to the space, not just renting "a projector and a mic." If you're not sure what your room needs, describe the space, the guest count, and what's happening — presentation, ceremony, panel — and get a recommendation before the day arrives.
