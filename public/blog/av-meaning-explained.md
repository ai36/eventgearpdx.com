# What Does A/V Mean? A Plain-English Guide for Event Planners

![black and green audio mixer - EventGear PDX](/blog/images/av-meaning-explained/cover.jpg)

> A/V stands for audio/visual — but the term covers more than most people expect. Here's what it actually includes and why it matters when you're planning an event.

You're booking a venue, writing an event budget, or reading a vendor contract, and the term "A/V" keeps showing up. Maybe the venue says "A/V not included," or a coordinator asks if you're bringing your own A/V. You have a general sense it means sound and screens, but you're not sure where the line is drawn — and that uncertainty matters once you're the one responsible for a room full of people actually hearing and seeing what's supposed to happen.

## So What Does A/V Actually Mean?

A/V is shorthand for "audio/visual" — anything that lets people hear or see content during an event. That's a wide net. It covers microphones and speakers, projectors and screens, laptops and video cables, and increasingly the cameras and streaming gear used to pipe a meeting to remote attendees on Zoom or Teams. If a piece of equipment exists to carry sound or image from one person to a room (or beyond it), it falls under A/V.

## The Term Is Vague by Design — and That's the Problem

Because "A/V" is such a broad label, two people can use it and mean completely different things. A venue that says it provides "basic A/V" might mean a projector and nothing else — no microphone, no speakers, no technician to run any of it. A client who asks a venue for "A/V" might assume that includes wireless mics for a panel discussion, when it doesn't. The word itself promises nothing specific; it just signals that *some* audio or visual equipment exists somewhere in the plan.

## Why the Ambiguity Costs You Later

This gap shows up at the worst possible time — usually the morning of the event, when someone discovers the "A/V" the venue promised is a wall-mounted TV with no HDMI cable, or a conference room speakerphone that can't fill a room of 60 people. A board presentation with no working microphone, a wedding toast nobody in the back row can hear, a hybrid meeting where remote attendees see a frozen screen — all of these trace back to one side assuming "A/V" meant more than it did.

## How to Use the Term So It Actually Protects You

The fix is simple: never leave "A/V" undefined in a booking conversation. Ask for an itemized list — how many microphones, what kind (handheld, lavalier, podium), what size screen, whether a technician will be present, and whether streaming to remote attendees is included. Once you have that list, you can compare what a venue offers against what your event actually needs and rent the gap separately.

## FAQ

**Is A/V the same as "audio-visual equipment"?**
Yes — A/V is simply the abbreviation for audio-visual, referring to any equipment used for sound or image during a presentation, meeting, or event.

**What's the difference between A/V and AV production?**
"A/V" usually refers to the equipment itself — mics, speakers, projectors. "AV production" or "event production" often implies a larger scope, including design elements like lighting or staging, which is outside what equipment rental alone covers.

**Does A/V include live streaming?**
Increasingly yes. Streaming a meeting or ceremony to remote attendees on Zoom or Teams requires a camera, audio feed, and often a dedicated technician — all considered part of modern A/V support.

**My venue says it has "in-house A/V" — do I still need to rent anything?**
Maybe. Ask exactly what's included and whether a technician operates it during your event. If the venue only provides a screen or a fixed speaker, you'll likely still need microphones or a proper PA system for a group larger than a handful of people.

**Why does a venue sometimes charge an outside-vendor fee for A/V?**
Some venues have exclusive in-house A/V contracts and charge a fee if you bring your own equipment or technician. Always confirm this with the venue before booking outside gear.

## The Bottom Line

"A/V" is a useful shorthand, but it's not a specification. Treat it as a placeholder until someone — a venue, a vendor, you — writes down exactly what equipment and support it includes. That one clarifying question, asked early, is what separates an event that runs smoothly from one where the microphone doesn't work five minutes before the first speaker walks up.
