# How to Turn Any Room Into a Working Meeting Room for a Day

![turned on LED projector on table - EventGear PDX](/blog/images/turn-any-room-into-meeting-room-for-a-day/cover.jpg)

> A practical guide to setting up an ordinary office, hall, or rented room for a one-day meeting, training, or presentation — what actually breaks and how to plan around it.

Your quarterly all-hands is in three weeks and the only space big enough is an open-plan office with the desks pushed back, or a plain rented room at a business center, or a community hall with folding chairs. There's no AV in the room — maybe a wall-mounted TV nobody past the fourth row can read. Nobody's going to install a permanent system for an event that happens once a quarter, and they shouldn't have to. The question is what that room actually needs to function for one day.

## The Room Was Never Built for This

A TV on a cart works for eight people around a table and fails the moment you add rows. The rule that matters is distance from screen to the furthest seat, not the front row — the back row decides the screen size. Past roughly 15 people, a projector and a properly sized screen beats any display you can wall-mount. Room light matters just as much: a wall of afternoon windows will wash out even a decent projector, so the wall you pick usually has to be the one without windows, which then decides which way the chairs face.

## Sound and Remote Attendees Fail Quietly

A presenter's voice carries fine to about 20 people in a normal room. At 40 or more, or in a space with hard floors and high ceilings, the back rows stop following without ever saying so. A panel needs one microphone per speaker, not a single handheld passed down the table, and floor Q&A needs its own mic or the question disappears for everyone, including anyone joining by Zoom or Teams. A laptop mic sitting on a conference table is the single most common point of failure on hybrid calls: the room hears the remote attendees fine, but the remote attendees hear an echo of the room.

## What This Costs You If It's Not Fixed

None of this fails loudly. Nobody stands up and says they can't hear — they just stop paying attention, stop asking questions, and check their phones. A board presentation where half the room missed the numbers, or a hybrid all-hands where remote staff got fifteen minutes of echo before muting themselves, doesn't look like an AV failure. It looks like a meeting that didn't land. The fix is almost never the content of the presentation — it's whether the room let people actually hear and see it.

## Match Equipment to the Actual Problem

Each issue above has a specific fix, not a general upgrade. A room past 15 people gets a projector sized to the furthest seat, on the wall away from windows. A room past 20 people, or one with hard surfaces, gets a small PA with one or two speakers on stands. Panels get individual microphones; open floor discussion gets a dedicated handheld. Hybrid sessions get proper audio pickup routed into the call, not a laptop mic, plus slides sent to both the room screen and the meeting software at once.

## Power, Cabling, and Timing Decide Whether It Holds Together

Most rented or borrowed rooms have one usable circuit along one wall. Running a projector, PA, laptop, and chargers off a single outlet behind a curtain is how a room goes dark mid-sentence. Any cable crossing a walkway needs to be taped down — it's a trip hazard and the fastest way to lose sound when a chair catches a cord. Setup also isn't a fifteen-minute job: testing needs to happen with the actual laptop and the actual slide deck, in the room, before anyone sits down, because that's when a missing adapter or a locked file gets caught instead of discovered live. This is why our technician arrives with time to test the full chain and stays on site for the entire event — not just to drop off gear.

## FAQ

**How many people does a room need before it needs a projector instead of a TV?**
Somewhere around 15 people is the practical line. Below that, a large TV can still work if the room is shallow. Past it, screen distance for the back row matters more than what's convenient to mount.

**Do we need a microphone if the presenter has a strong voice?**
It depends on room size and surfaces more than the presenter. A confident voice carries to about 20 people in a quiet room with soft surfaces; hard floors, glass, and high ceilings shrink that number fast.

**What's the biggest risk for a hybrid meeting with remote attendees?**
Audio pickup, not video. A laptop mic on the table creates echo for remote attendees and leaves in-room questions inaudible to them — dedicated microphones routed into the call fix both.

**How much lead time does a one-day setup like this need?**
As much as you can give it. Availability depends on the schedule, and testing with your actual laptop and slide deck takes real time before the room needs to be ready — booking as soon as the date is set avoids a scramble.

## The Takeaway

A room doesn't need to be a conference room to work for a day — it needs a clear sightline to the screen, sound that reaches the back row, power that won't cut out mid-presentation, and someone who tested the whole chain before the first person sat down. Get those right and an open-plan office or a rented hall does the job just as well as a dedicated meeting room.
